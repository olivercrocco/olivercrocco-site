I am starting to volunteer with an employment skills class in Louisiana for formerly incarcerated individuals. Some of these folks went in at 18 and are coming out after 20 or 30 years, so there's a lot to catch up on.

The class covers what you would expect: how to dress for an interview, how to be on time, how to make a good first impression, and how to write a resume. What struck me was how much the world of work changed while they were inside. Most job applications are online now. Many employers use software to screen resumes before a person ever reads them. Someone who went in before smartphones now needs an email address, a phone number, and a password just to apply.

So I started building small tools for the class. There are four so far. One builds a resume, one helps people practice for an interview, one plans a first paycheck, and one helps write a letter about a record to a landlord, an employer, or a licensing board. They are free and work without the internet. They keep nothing a person types, they are written in plain language, and they can read each step out loud. They are collected on my [Reentry Toolkit](/reentry/) page.

Part of my interest comes from my field. I study human and workforce development, which is about how people build skills and put them to work. People coming home from prison are often left out of that conversation. Louisiana's prisons teach welding, carpentry, HVAC, culinary arts, and dozens of other trades to more than 5,000 people a year. Many of the people in this class have worked for decades inside, in kitchens, maintenance shops, and fields. That is real work experience. Louisiana employers need skilled workers. Too often that experience disappears from view the day someone walks out.

The other part of my interests is that it's simply the right thing to do. In October I toured the Louisiana State Penitentiary at Angola. Afterward I wrote [a poem](/poetry/angola/) about what I saw there. Most of the men I met are serving life for one thing they did once. I believe everyone deserves a second chance. A person who went in at 18 and comes home at 48 still has a lot of life to live. Work is a big part of building that life.

These tools are a small piece of that. They will not get anyone hired on their own. I hope they make the first weeks home a little less daunting. I also hope other programs will use them or adapt them for their own states. If you work with people coming home and want to try them, I would like to hear from you. We could also adapt to other states and contexts.

Onward.
